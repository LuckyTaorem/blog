## Romero’s Early Career and the Road to “Hungry Wives”

George A. Romero is a name synonymous with the modern zombie mythos. After the groundbreaking *Night of the Living Dead* (1968) and the politically charged *The Crazies* (1973), Romero’s reputation rested on his ability to blend social commentary with visceral horror. Yet, between those milestones he ventured into territory far removed from the undead: low‑budget exploitation cinema.

Two lesser‑known titles illustrate this detour:

* **There’s Always Vanilla** – a semi‑autobiographical road‑movie that never reached mainstream audiences.  
* **Season of the Witch** – originally shot under the working title *Hungry Wives* and marketed as soft‑core erotica.

Understanding Romero’s motivations requires a look at the 1970s independent film ecosystem. Distribution channels were fragmented; producers often re‑branded a film to chase the most lucrative niche—be it grindhouse, drive‑in, or adult theaters. Romero, still establishing his voice, accepted a modest budget and a contract that promised a quick return if the film could be sold as “exploitation.”

## The Production of “Hungry Wives” – From Script to Screen

### Concept and Script

The screenplay for *Hungry Wives* was drafted by a writer familiar with the “sex‑ploitation” formula: a thin plot, a handful of titillating set‑pieces, and plenty of dialogue to pad runtime. The story centered on a suburban housewife who discovers a secret coven of witches. The premise was deliberately sensational, aiming to attract audiences looking for supernatural intrigue mixed with erotic overtones.

### Shooting Constraints

* **Budget:** Roughly $30,000 (adjusted for inflation, still a shoestring).  
* **Schedule:** 12 days of principal photography, typical for grindhouse productions.  
* **Locations:** A single suburban house, a nearby woods, and a makeshift “ritual” set built in a garage.

Romero’s crew employed practical lighting and handheld 35 mm cameras to keep costs low. The limited resources forced creative solutions—most notably, the film’s single sex scene was shot in a single take, using natural light to avoid expensive set‑ups.

### Post‑Production Choices

Editing was performed on a flatbed editor, a labor‑intensive process that left little room for reshoots. Romero’s background in horror editing meant he prioritized pacing over visual polish. The final cut ran just under 80 minutes, a length that fit the double‑feature programming model of the era.

## Marketing Missteps: Why “Hungry Wives” Flopped

### The Exploitation Promise vs. Reality

The film was advertised in trade papers as a soft‑core pornographic experience. Posters featured suggestive taglines and a silhouette of a witch’s hand reaching for a lover. However, the actual content delivered only **one** brief sex scene, with the rest of the runtime dominated by dialogue‑heavy scenes that resembled a low‑budget drama.

### Audience Expectations

Grindhouse patrons in 1973 expected:

* Frequent nudity or explicit content.  
* Fast‑paced, sensational action.  
* Minimal dialogue.

*Hungry Wives* failed on all three counts. The mismatch led to immediate walk‑outs, and theater owners quickly pulled the film from their schedules.

### Distribution Fallout

The film’s distributor attempted to re‑brand it as *Season of the Witch* later that year, hoping the occult angle would attract a different crowd. The re‑title change came too late; the initial box‑office numbers were already a loss, and the film never secured a wide theatrical run.

## Industry Impact and the Cult Resurgence

### Influence on Exploitation Filmmaking

Romero’s brief foray into exploitation demonstrated that even visionary directors could misjudge market signals. The film became a cautionary case study in film schools, illustrating the importance of aligning creative intent with audience demand.

### Legacy and Modern Reception

Since its restoration, *Season of the Witch* has been featured at several genre‑film festivals, including the **Midnight Madness** program at the Toronto International Film Festival and the **Fantastic Fest** in Austin. Critics now highlight the film’s surprisingly subversive themes:

* **Feminist undertones** – The narrative centers on a housewife who discovers agency through an occult sisterhood, a motif that resonates with today’s discussions about women’s empowerment in horror cinema.  
* **Atmospheric tension** – Despite the limited budget, Romero’s use of shadow and sound design creates a lingering sense of dread that foreshadows his later, more polished works.  
* **Cult‑aesthetic charm** – The grainy 35 mm texture and the film’s “so‑bad‑it’s‑good” reputation have earned it a place alongside other beloved grindhouse relics such as *The Last House on the Left* (1972) and *I Spit on Your Grave* (1978).

## Conclusion

*Season of the Witch* stands as a fascinating footnote in George A. Romero’s storied career—a reminder that even visionary filmmakers can stumble when they chase market trends without fully understanding the audience they aim to serve. The film’s resurrection through meticulous restoration and its subsequent embrace by niche‑circuit audiences illustrate a broader truth about art: commercial failure does not equate to cultural irrelevance. By re‑examining *Hungry Wives* through a modern lens, we uncover a work that, while imperfect, contributes to the tapestry of 1970s exploitation cinema and enriches the dialogue around gender, power, and the occult in horror.
