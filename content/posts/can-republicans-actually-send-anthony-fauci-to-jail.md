## The Contempt Conundrum: Fifth Amendment vs Presidential Pardon

When former White House chief medical advisor Anthony Fau appeared before the Senate Committee on Health, Education, Labor, and Pensions, he invoked the Fifth Amendment over 100 times. His silence was not a mere political stunt; it was a legal shield against self‑incrimination. Yet, the same silence became a rallying point for Republican lawmakers who argue that a pre‑emptive pardon from President Biden—covering federal crimes from 2014 to 2025—should strip Fau of his Fifth Amendment privilege.

The core of the dispute lies in the intersection of two constitutional doctrines:

1. **The Fifth Amendment** protects individuals from being compelled to testify against themselves in criminal proceedings.
2. **The presidential pardon** is a constitutional power that absolves individuals of federal criminal liability but does not automatically negate constitutional rights.

Legal scholars such as Aziz Huq and former U.S. Attorney Barbara Mc Quade have clarified that a pardon does not retroactively cover actions taken after the pardon’s issuance, nor does it erase the right to refuse to answer questions that could lead to criminal prosecution. In Fau ’s case, the questions posed by the committee concern potential perjury or omission during the hearing itself—conduct that falls outside the pardon’s temporal scope.

The Senate’s contempt power, which can impose up to one year in jail and a $100,000 fine, hinges on whether Fau  can be held liable for refusing to comply with a congressional subpoena. The legal debate centers on whether the pardon shields him from contempt charges, or whether the Fifth Amendment remains intact despite the pardon.

## Legal Precedents and the 1896 Supreme Court Case

Senator Rand Paul has invoked an 1896 Supreme Court decision to argue that a presidential pardon automatically removes Fifth Amendment rights. The case, *United States v. Baker*, held that a pardon could extinguish the right to refuse to testify in certain contexts. However, the Court’s reasoning was narrowly tailored to the specific facts of that era and did not address the modern interplay between pardons and congressional contempt.

Modern jurisprudence suggests that the pardon’s scope is limited to the specific offenses it names. The *Baker* precedent does not extend to the broader constitutional guarantee of the Fifth Amendment. Moreover, the Supreme Court has consistently upheld the Fifth Amendment’s protection against self‑incrimination, even in the face of executive clemency.

The Senate’s position is that the pardon does not preclude a contempt proceeding. If the committee can prove that Fau  deliberately obstructed the inquiry, the contempt charge could proceed regardless of the pardon. The 60‑vote threshold on the Senate floor remains a significant hurdle, but the political momentum behind the charge could push the vote toward that threshold.

## The Senate’s Contempt Power and Voting Threshold

Contempt of Congress is a serious tool that can be wielded against individuals who obstruct legislative investigations. The penalty—up to one year in prison and a $100,000 fine—serves both as punishment and deterrence. However, the Senate’s procedural rules require a supermajority of 60 votes to convict an individual of contempt.

The current composition of the Senate, with a narrow Republican majority, makes the 60‑vote threshold a realistic target. Yet, the political calculus is complex: a conviction could be perceived as partisan overreach, potentially galvanizing public opinion against the Republican agenda. Conversely, a failure to secure the 60 votes could embolden Fau  and other officials to continue invoking the Fifth Amendment in future investigations.

The Senate’s decision will set a precedent for how presidential pardons interact with congressional oversight. If the Senate proceeds with a contempt charge, it will reinforce the principle that a pardon does not grant immunity from all forms of legal accountability, especially when the conduct in question is ongoing or future.

## State-Level Pursuits: Florida’s Investigation

While the federal debate rages, state-level actions are also underway. Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier announced a state investigation into Fau , citing concerns over potential violations of state law. The investigation is distinct from the federal contempt proceeding but underscores the multi‑layered nature of legal scrutiny.

State investigations can target a broader range of offenses, including those not covered by federal pardons. If the Florida investigation uncovers evidence of wrongdoing, it could lead to state criminal charges that the federal pardon does not shield. This dual‑track approach—federal contempt and state criminal inquiry—creates a complex legal environment for Fau .

The precedent set by Steve Bannon’s pardon illustrates the limits of executive clemency. Bannon was pardoned for fraud charges related to the “We Build the Wall” campaign but was later sentenced to four months in jail for contempt of Congress over the January 6 Capitol attack. The distinction lies in the specificity of the pardon: it covered only the fraud charges, not the contempt allegations. Fau ’ situation mirrors this scenario, where the pardon’s scope is limited to federal crimes from 2014 to 2025, leaving open the possibility of contempt charges for current conduct.

## Broader Implications for Congressional Oversight

The Fau  case is more than a legal footnote; it reflects the evolving dynamics between the executive branch, Congress, and the judiciary. Key implications include:

- **Presidential Pardon Limits**: The case clarifies that pardons cannot be used as a blanket shield against all forms of legal accountability, especially for ongoing or future conduct.
- **Congressional Authority**: A successful contempt charge would reinforce Congress’s power to compel testimony and hold officials accountable, even in the face of executive clemency.
- **Political Polarization**: The debate risks deepening partisan divides, as Republicans may view the contempt charge as a necessary check on the Biden administration, while Democrats may see it as a politically motivated attack.
- **Legal Precedent**: The outcome will influence future cases involving presidential pardons and congressional investigations, potentially reshaping the balance of power among the branches of government.

The intersection of constitutional rights, executive clemency, and congressional oversight will continue to be a flashpoint in American politics. As the Senate deliberates, the legal community watches closely for a decision that could redefine the scope of presidential pardons and the limits of the Fifth Amendment.

## FAQ

### Q: Does a presidential pardon remove the Fifth Amendment right to refuse testimony?
**A:** No. A pardon absolves the individual of specific federal criminal liability but does not negate constitutional protections against self‑incrimination.

### Q: What is the maximum penalty for contempt of Congress?
**A:** Up to one year in prison and a fine of up to $100,000.

### Q: How many votes are required to convict someone of contempt in the Senate?
**A:** A supermajority of 60 votes is required.

### Q: Can a state investigation proceed even if the individual has a federal pardon?
**A:** Yes. State investigations target state law violations, which are not covered by federal pardons.

### Q: What precedent exists for a pardon not covering contempt charges?
**A:** Steve Bannon was pardoned for fraud charges but later sentenced for contempt of Congress, illustrating that pardons can be narrowly tailored.

## Conclusion

The clash between Anthony Fau’s Fifth Amendment invocations and the Senate’s contempt powers underscores a pivotal moment in U.S. constitutional law. The outcome will determine whether a presidential pardon can shield an individual from congressional oversight and set a lasting precedent for the interplay between executive clemency and legislative authority. As the Senate approaches its vote, the nation watches, aware that the decision will reverberate through the corridors of power for years to come.
